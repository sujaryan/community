# Red Flags & Personal Process

## Moats and management (the qualitative edge)

**Moats** — durable competitive advantages. Five classic sources, with Indian illustrations:
- **Intangibles / brand** — HUL, Asian Paints, Colgate (pricing power).
- **Switching costs** — TCS/Infosys (ripping out core systems is costly/risky → repeat business).
- **Network effects** — platforms where each user adds value for others.
- **Cost advantage** — scale-driven low-cost producers.
- **Efficient scale** — markets large enough for only a few profitable players.

The quantitative tell of a real moat is **ROCE/ROE that stays high for many years while the business grows**. Always ask what could *kill* the moat (technology, regulation, new entrants).

**Management quality** — read several years of annual-report letters and concalls. Did they do what they said? Is guidance realistic or perennially over-promised? How do they communicate in bad times? Study the **capital-allocation track record**: sensible reinvestment and rational dividends/buybacks vs empire-building, value-destroying M&A.

## Governance and promoter red flags

- **Promoter holding** — high, stable ownership = skin in the game. A persistent quarter-on-quarter *decline* is a warning.
- **Promoter pledging** — promoter shares used as loan collateral is the hidden time bomb of Indian markets. Rough scale: 0% ideal; 1–10% low (monitor); 10–30% caution (ask why); **above 30% is a serious red flag** — a falling price can trigger forced lender selling and a death spiral. Check NSE/BSE pledge data every quarter.
- **Related-party transactions, board independence, audit-committee strength** round out the check.

## Behavioral traps

- **Chasing recent IPOs and momentum.** Base rate is sobering: in a study of 377 Indian IPOs (2005–2015), only ~10% beat the benchmark over 36 months, with large average underperformance. Listing-day pops and long-term value are different games.
- **Anchoring** — fixating on a past price ("it was ₹500, now ₹300, so it's cheap") or your own purchase price.
- **Confirmation bias** — seeking only supporting evidence. Force yourself to write the bear case.
- **Over-reliance on management guidance and GMP** — GMP is unofficial, unregulated sentiment, not valuation.

## IPO-specific risks (for recently-listed / run-up stocks)

- **OFS-heavy issues** — existing investors cashing out rather than fresh growth capital going in.
- **Pre-IPO revenue spikes** that flatter the latest year.
- **Short consolidated operating history** after a recent acquisition.
- **Customer/partner concentration.**
- **Lock-in expiries** that flood supply months after listing.

The "easy listing gain" is gone once a stock has already popped; buying after a sharp run is a momentum entry at an elevated level, not a margin-of-safety entry.

## The personal-process toolkit

The repeatable habits matter more than any single insight. They're what separate investing from gambling.

**Investment checklist (Munger-style)** — a fixed set of pass/fail questions answered *every* time: Do I understand the business? ROCE durably >15%? CFO/net-profit healthy over 5 years? Debt manageable? Receivable days stable? Promoter pledge low? Any auditor/RPT red flags? Valuation reasonable vs growth and quality? Borrow someone's checklist, not their risk tolerance.

**Research journal** — for every decision record date, price, thesis, data relied on, reasoning. Reviewed later, it's the only honest way to learn whether you were right *for the right reasons* or merely lucky.

**One-page thesis** — (1) what the business is, (2) the 2–3 core drivers, (3) valuation you'll pay and expected return, (4) key risks, (5) **invalidation conditions** (the specific events that make you sell). When price moves, return to it: did the business change, or just the price?

**Watchlist** — curated (~10–25 names), each with a one-line thesis, a buy price/valuation, upcoming catalysts (results dates), and an invalidation trigger. Do the research when there's no urgency so corrections become opportunities, not panics. Review lightly weekly, deeply each quarter around results.

## Position sizing and portfolio construction

- **How many stocks:** roughly **12–20** captures most diversifiable (unsystematic) risk while staying trackable. (Lynch suggested 3–10 for individuals; Greenblatt runs 5–8 concentrated; Mukherjea's Coffee Can equal-weights ~10–25 quality names; Zerodha Varsity cites ~21 as the point of diminishing diversification.)
- **Max per position:** cap single-stock weight (a common rule is ~10–15% max) so one idiosyncratic blow-up can't sink you. (Note: Zerodha's "5% rule" is a *per-trade risk* rule for traders, not a holding cap — don't conflate.)
- **Conviction sizing:** Greenblatt's "look down, not up" — largest weights go to lowest-downside / highest-conviction ideas, not highest-upside lottery tickets.
- **Lump sum vs staggered:** lump-sum usually wins mathematically (markets rise more often than they fall), but staggering reduces timing regret and is more defensible for volatile mid/small-caps. Staggering a *single stock* is riskier than staggering an index, because one business can be permanently impaired.
- **Volatility awareness:** size down inherently more-volatile names so no single position can blow a hole in the portfolio.

## Recommended learning resources

**Global classics:** *The Intelligent Investor* (Graham); *One Up on Wall Street* / *Beating the Street* (Lynch); *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (Fisher); *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (Munger); *The Psychology of Money* (Housel); Aswath Damodaran's free valuation materials.

**Indian-context:** *Coffee Can Investing* / *The Unusual Billionaires* (Mukherjea); *What I Learned About Investing from Darwin* (Pulak Prasad); *Stocks to Riches* (Parag Parikh); *Bulls, Bears and Other Beasts* (Santosh Nair); *Mastering the Art of Stock Investing* (Vijay Malik); *The Long Game* (Khandelwal).

**Free websites:** **Zerodha Varsity** (best free structured curriculum — start here); **Safal Niveshak** (process, checklists, temperament); Screener.in blog, Trade Brains, Finology; CRISIL/ICRA methodology docs for sector frameworks. The most underrated "course": read a few dozen annual reports and concall transcripts and patterns emerge.
