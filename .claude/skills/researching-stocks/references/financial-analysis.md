# Financial Analysis (Beyond the Basics)

## Read the three statements as one system

- **Income statement** → profit. Flows to retained earnings on the balance sheet and is the starting line of the cash-flow statement.
- **Balance sheet** → snapshot of assets, liabilities, equity. Its receivables/inventory/payables drive the working-capital lines of cash flow.
- **Cash-flow statement** → the truth-teller. A company can report profit and still bleed cash.

Profit without cash is suspect. Growth not funded by cash must be funded by the balance sheet (debt or equity dilution) — so always check *how* growth was paid for.

## Key ratios and what they reveal

**Profitability**
- **ROE** = net profit ÷ equity. **ROCE** = EBIT ÷ capital employed. Sustained ROCE above ~15% is the quantitative fingerprint of a quality business / moat.
- **DuPont** splits ROE into margin × asset turnover × leverage — so you know whether returns come from genuine efficiency or just debt.
- Operating and net margins, and their *trend*, matter more than the absolute level.

**Leverage / solvency**
- **Debt-to-equity.**
- **Interest coverage** = EBIT ÷ interest. Below ~2–3x is a distress warning.
- **Debt/EBITDA.** Above ~3x is leveraged; above ~5x is dangerous.

**Efficiency**
- **Debtor (receivable) days, inventory days, cash-conversion cycle.** Rising debtor days is a classic revenue-recognition red flag.

**Liquidity**
- Current and quick ratios.

## The single most important check: cash flow vs reported profit

Compute cumulative **operating cash flow (CFO)** vs cumulative **net profit** over 5 years. A healthy company converts the bulk of profit into cash. A CFO/net-profit ratio persistently below ~0.6–0.8 demands investigation before buying.

*Why this matters (real case):* At Manpasand Beverages, analysts flagged operating cash flow lagging reported profit years before the collapse; the statutory auditor (Deloitte) later resigned mid-term citing information not provided, and the stock roughly halved over two sessions. A forensic audit found shell companies faking sales. An investor tracking CFO-vs-profit and receivables-vs-revenue had a multi-quarter head start.

## Read multi-year trends, not snapshots

One good year proves nothing. Look for consistency and direction across 5–10 years.

## Accounting red flags and basic forensic checks

- **Receivables growing faster than revenue** → possible fictitious or aggressively-booked sales.
- **Operating cash flow persistently lagging net profit.**
- **Frequent equity dilution** (rising share count) — check whether per-share value is actually growing.
- **Large or opaque related-party transactions** — a small share with clear rationale is fine; large or unexplained is not.
- **Auditor resignation or qualified / "emphasis of matter" opinion** — one of the most powerful single red flags; a reputable auditor walking away mid-term often means they wouldn't sign.
- **Frequent changes to accounting policies** (revenue recognition, depreciation, inventory) — honest companies rarely change these.
- **Large year-end adjustments, frequent restatements**, or numbers that differ across bodies the company reports to.
- **High CWIP** (capital work-in-progress) that never converts to productive assets; capex persistently far above depreciation with no revenue to show for it.
- **Beneish M-Score** (optional advanced screen) — an eight-variable manipulation model; a score above roughly −1.78 flags elevated manipulation probability.

## Thresholds that should change the conclusion

- Auditor resignation or qualified opinion → pass/exit.
- CFO/net-profit persistently below ~0.6 → investigate before buying.
- ROCE durably falling below ~12–15% for a "quality" thesis → thesis weakening.
- Debtor days rising materially faster than revenue → red flag.

These are rules of thumb, not laws — "good" levels vary by sector and cycle. Always compare like with like and across multiple years.
