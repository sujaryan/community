# Valuation

A business is worth the present value of the cash it will generate for its owners. Everything below is a tool for approximating that. The output is always a *range*, not a point — demand a margin of safety.

## Relative valuation (start here)

- **P/E** = price ÷ earnings. Compare to genuine peers *and* the stock's own 5–10-year median. Useless for loss-making companies.
- **P/B** = price ÷ book value. Essential for banks, NBFCs, insurers (book value is the productive asset base). Largely irrelevant for asset-light businesses.
- **EV/EBITDA** = enterprise value ÷ EBITDA. Better than P/E for capital-intensive businesses and for comparing companies with different debt loads.
- **PEG** = P/E ÷ growth rate. A rough check on whether a high P/E is "earned" by growth. ~1 is often called fair, but treat it as a sense-check, not gospel.
- **P/Sales** — fallback for fast-growing, not-yet-profitable companies. Use with great caution; it ignores whether profit is achievable.

## How to do peer comparison properly

1. Select genuine peers — same business model, size band, geography.
2. Compute the peer **median** (resists outliers better than the average).
3. Adjust for differences in growth, margins, and quality. A company deserves a **premium** multiple for higher ROCE, faster durable growth, lower leverage, cleaner governance — and a **discount** for the reverse.

## Intrinsic value / DCF thinking (accessible version)

No 50-row spreadsheet needed. Think in three parts:
1. How fast can free cash flow grow, and for how long?
2. How certain is that growth (quality / moat)?
3. What return do you demand (discount rate — higher for riskier businesses)?

The result is acutely sensitive to assumptions, so run **optimistic / base / pessimistic** cases and buy meaningfully below your estimate (margin of safety). Even "intrinsic" values rest on assumptions ultimately benchmarked to the market — so combine relative and intrinsic views rather than trusting either alone.

## Cheap vs expensive — the real question

A low multiple can be a bargain *or* a value trap (cheap because the business is deteriorating). A high multiple can be justified by quality *or* be a momentum trap. Never ask "is the P/E low?" Ask "is the price low relative to the cash this business will durably generate?"

## Sector-specific valuation nuances

- **Banks / NBFCs** → **P/B adjusted for ROE** (a low-ROE lender deserves a low P/B) and P/E. Never EV/EBITDA.
- **FMCG / consumer** → high P/E and P/B are structural (brand value isn't on the balance sheet). Judge vs the company's own history and volume growth.
- **IT services** → P/E and EV/EBITDA, with heavy weight on growth visibility and margins.
- **Capital-intensive / cyclicals** → EV/EBITDA over a full cycle. Beware the **cyclical trap**: a *low* trailing P/E near a cycle top often signals peak (about-to-fall) earnings.
